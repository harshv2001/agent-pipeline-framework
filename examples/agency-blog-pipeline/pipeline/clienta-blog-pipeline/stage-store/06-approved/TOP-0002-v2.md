## What was drafted

<p>Building a 13-week cash flow forecast comes down to four steps: list
what's coming in, list what's going out, roll both forward week by week
in a simple grid, and update it every week as real numbers replace
estimates. Here's what each step actually looks like in practice.</p>

<h2>Step 1: List what's coming in, by week</h2>
<p>Go through every invoice you've sent that's still unpaid and sort it
by its actual expected payment date, not its due date — if a client
reliably pays two weeks late, put it where it'll really land. Add any
recurring revenue (retainers, subscriptions) in the week it actually
hits your account. A 15-person services business typically ends up with
10–25 line items across 13 weeks; if you have far more than that, group
by client rather than by invoice.</p>

<h2>Step 2: List what's going out, by week</h2>
<p>Payroll is usually your biggest, most predictable outflow — put it on
its exact pay dates, not "once a month." Then add rent, loan payments,
and recurring software costs on their actual due dates, and estimate
variable costs (contractors, ad spend, one-off purchases) using your
last three months as a baseline. The most common mistake here is
forgetting quarterly or annual costs — a tax payment or an annual
software renewal that only happens once a year still needs its own line
in whichever week it falls.</p>

<h2>Step 3: Roll it forward in a weekly grid</h2>
<p>Put 13 columns (one per week) and three rows: cash in, cash out, and
running balance. The running balance for week 2 is week 1's ending
balance plus week 2's in, minus week 2's out — carry it forward the same
way across all 13 weeks. This is the one number that matters most: the
week where that running balance gets uncomfortably low is the week
you'll actually feel a cash crunch, often several weeks before it shows
up in your monthly P&amp;L.</p>

<h2>Step 4: Update it weekly, not monthly</h2>
<p>Every week, replace your estimates for that week with what actually
happened, and re-check weeks 10–13 against anything new you've learned
(a client who paid late, a cost that came in higher than expected). A
forecast updated monthly is already stale by the time you look at it
again — weekly is what keeps the week-6 or week-9 warning sign visible
while you still have time to act on it.</p>
